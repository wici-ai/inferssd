# Tessaline: Design and Operations of the Orrin Valley Rail Telemetry Store

Revision 7, prepared by the Platform Storage Group of the Orrin Valley Transit Authority

## 1. Background

The Orrin Valley network runs 214 kilometres of double track between the port of Calder Reach and the upland towns around Mirrow Lake. Every one of its 61 electric trainsets carries roughly 1,900 sensors: axle-box thermometers, pantograph force gauges, door controllers, traction inverter monitors, brake cylinder pressure transducers and a few dozen accelerometers bolted to the bogie frames. Until the last refit, the readings were written to removable flash cards that depot staff collected once a week. A failing bearing therefore announced itself, at best, seven days after the first warning sign, and twice in that period the first sign anyone noticed was smoke.

Tessaline is the storage service that replaced the flash cards. It receives a continuous stream from every trainset over the cellular modems fitted during the refit, keeps eighteen months of full-resolution history online, and answers the queries that maintenance planners, the control room and two external research partners send it. This document describes how the service is built, why the main decisions were made, what has gone wrong in its first two years of operation, and which problems remain open. It is written for engineers who will change the system, so it spends more time on trade-offs and failure behaviour than on configuration.

The original requirements were short. Readings had to be durable within two seconds of leaving a train, because the control room wanted to act on brake faults before the next station. A planner had to be able to pull a year of axle temperatures for one bogie in under ten seconds. The system had to survive the loss of either of the authority's two data halls without losing acknowledged data. Finally, it had to fit within a hardware budget that the finance office described, not unkindly, as whatever the old ticketing servers had been costing.

## 2. Data model

A reading in Tessaline is a triple of a series key, a timestamp and a value. The series key names the physical source: trainset number, car position, subsystem, sensor and channel, packed into a 64-bit identifier by the onboard gateway. Timestamps are microseconds since the authority's epoch, taken from the satellite-disciplined clock in each gateway. Values are 32-bit floats for most analogue channels and small integers for door states, fault codes and counters. We considered storing strings for fault descriptions, but the onboard firmware already maps every fault to a numeric code, and a single lookup table in the query layer turned out to be enough.

Series are grouped into what we call shelves. A shelf holds every series from one car over one calendar day, which in practice means between 280 and 340 series and about 41 million readings. The shelf is the unit of placement, replication, compaction and deletion. Choosing it was the most consequential decision in the project. A shelf per sensor would have produced roughly forty million tiny objects a year and swamped the metadata service; a shelf per trainset per day would have made the common query, one bogie over many months, read fourteen times more data than it needed. One car per day matches both the way the depots think about maintenance and the way the hardware is wired, since each car has its own gateway.

Late data is the main complication. Trains lose coverage in the Brackwell cutting and in the three long tunnels under the Sennet ridge, and the gateways buffer up to six hours of readings while they wait. When coverage returns, a gateway sends the backlog interleaved with live readings, oldest first. Tessaline accepts readings for any shelf that is less than eight days old; older readings are written to a separate quarantine area where an operator decides whether to merge them. In two years the quarantine has received data on exactly four occasions, each time from a gateway whose clock had been set to the wrong year during a repair.

## 3. Ingest path

Each gateway holds a long-lived connection to one of six ingest nodes, chosen by a hash of its trainset number and re-chosen only when the node stops answering. Messages arrive in frames of up to 4,096 readings, compressed with a dictionary trained on the previous month's traffic. The ingest node decompresses a frame, checks the per-frame checksum and the sequence number, and appends the raw frame to a write-ahead log on local flash before it does anything else. Only after the log write has been flushed and copied to a peer node in the other data hall does the node acknowledge the frame. That copy is the reason the two-second durability target is met with room to spare: the median time from the train's radio to the acknowledgement is 310 milliseconds, of which about 240 are spent on the cellular network.

After acknowledgement, frames are decoded into readings and inserted into an in-memory buffer per shelf. When the buffer reaches 64 megabytes, or when a shelf has been idle for ten minutes, it is written out as an immutable segment and the corresponding log entries become eligible for deletion. Segments are columnar. Timestamps are stored as deltas of deltas, which for sensors that report at a fixed rate compresses most of them to a single bit, and values are encoded with an exclusive-or scheme that exploits the fact that consecutive floats from the same sensor usually share their leading bits. Across the fleet the average reading costs 1.7 bytes on disk, against 14 bytes in the raw frames.

The ingest nodes are deliberately simple. They do not interpret readings beyond decoding them, they hold no state that cannot be rebuilt from the write-ahead log, and they never talk to each other except to mirror logs. When an ingest node fails, its gateways reconnect elsewhere within about forty seconds and resend any frames that were not acknowledged, while the peer that held the mirrored log turns its contents into segments. Duplicate frames are recognised by their sequence numbers and dropped.

## 4. Storage layout and compaction

Segments live on a pool of eighteen storage nodes, nine in each data hall. Each node has twelve hard drives for cold data and four flash devices that hold recent segments and all of the indexes. A shelf is assigned to three nodes, two in one hall and one in the other, by a placement service that balances free space and recent query load. Placement is recorded in a small metadata store that runs a consensus protocol across five voters; it holds roughly 900,000 shelf records and fits comfortably in memory.

A busy shelf accumulates dozens of segments during its first day, because buffers are flushed on a timer as well as on size. Reading many small segments is slow, so a compaction process merges them. Compaction in Tessaline happens in three stages that follow the life of a shelf. During the first day, segments are merged whenever there are more than eight of them, producing larger segments that stay on flash. After the eight-day window for late data closes, the shelf is sealed: all of its segments are merged into one, the result is re-encoded with a slower but tighter compression setting, and the sealed shelf is moved to the hard drives. Finally, after eighteen months, a sealed shelf is downsampled to one-minute summaries and the full-resolution copy is deleted, except for shelves that a maintenance engineer has flagged as evidence for an investigation.

The sealing step is where most of the space is saved. Re-encoding with a larger block size and a stronger entropy coder reduces a typical shelf by a further 38 percent, but it costs about four seconds of processor time per shelf, which is why it is not done earlier. Sealing runs at night, when query load is lowest, and is throttled so that it never takes more than a quarter of a node's disk bandwidth. On a normal night the cluster seals between 1,400 and 1,500 shelves.

## 5. Query path

Most queries ask for a handful of series over a time range, sometimes with a simple aggregation such as a maximum per hour. A query node receives the request, looks up which shelves overlap the requested range, and fans out sub-queries to one replica of each shelf, preferring the replica in the same hall and the node with the shortest queue. Each shelf keeps a small index that maps every series to the byte ranges of its columns in each segment, so a storage node can read only the columns it needs. For a sealed shelf that index is about 30 kilobytes and is cached in memory on every node that holds a replica.

Long-range queries are dominated by the number of shelves they touch. A year of one bogie's axle temperatures crosses 365 shelves on hard drives, and a cold read of each costs one seek for the index if it is not cached plus one or two seeks for the data. With the work spread over eighteen nodes and 216 drives, the measured time for that query is between 3.1 and 4.6 seconds, comfortably inside the ten-second target. The worst case is a query that asks for every sensor on a whole trainset over several months, which research partners occasionally do. Such a query reads tens of gigabytes; rather than let it compete with interactive work, the query layer moves any request estimated to read more than two gigabytes to a batch queue that runs on a reserved slice of each node.

We added summary columns late in the project, after watching how planners actually used the system. For every series, a sealed shelf now stores the minimum, maximum, mean and count for each ten-minute window. A query that only needs hourly or daily figures reads the summaries and never touches the raw columns, which cut the median latency of the planning dashboards from 2.2 seconds to 190 milliseconds. The summaries add about three percent to the size of a sealed shelf.

## 6. Replication and failure handling

Three replicas across two halls mean that losing a whole hall leaves at least one replica of every shelf. It does not leave two, and for a few hours after a hall failure the surviving replicas are the only copies. The placement service therefore treats a hall failure differently from a node failure. When a single node disappears, its shelves are re-replicated from the survivors at a modest rate, because the cluster still has two copies of everything and there is no reason to flood the network. When a hall disappears, the placement service raises the repair rate, suspends sealing and downsampling, and asks the query layer to reject batch work until a second copy of every recent shelf exists again.

Replicas of an unsealed shelf are not identical byte for byte, because each storage node flushes and merges segments on its own schedule. They are identical in content, which is what matters, and a background checker compares content digests of every shelf once a week. Sealed shelves are identical byte for byte, which makes the check cheaper and makes it possible to rebuild a lost replica by copying a file instead of replaying data.

The write-ahead logs on the ingest nodes protect data that has been acknowledged but not yet turned into segments. Each log is mirrored to a peer in the other hall before acknowledgement, and log entries are deleted only after all three replicas of the corresponding shelf report that the segment containing them is durable. The rule sounds conservative, and it is: during a long storage outage the logs grow, and we sized the ingest nodes' flash so that they can hold thirty hours of fleet traffic, which at present is about 2.8 terabytes.

## 7. Capacity and measured performance

The fleet produces on average 52,000 readings per second, with peaks of about 190,000 when trains leave the tunnels together during the morning rush and their gateways empty their buffers. Six ingest nodes handle the peak at roughly a third of their processor capacity; we kept six rather than four so that one hall can carry the whole load. On disk, eighteen months of full-resolution data occupies 410 terabytes after replication, and the one-minute summaries that outlive it add about nine terabytes a year.

The most useful capacity figure turned out to be not storage but seeks. Hard drives deliver a roughly fixed number of random reads per second, and both sealing and long queries consume them. We now model each node as a budget of seeks per second shared between compaction, repair and queries, and the placement service tries to keep every node's projected seek demand under seventy percent of its budget. When the model predicts that a node will exceed it, for example because a research partner has scheduled a large export, sealing on that node is deferred to the following night.

## 8. Testing and validation

Before Tessaline carried live traffic, it ran for five months in parallel with the flash cards. Every card collected at the depots was read into a separate comparison store, and a nightly job compared it with what Tessaline had received over the air for the same car and day. The comparison was strict: every reading had to be present in both places with the same timestamp and the same value, and any difference was recorded together with the gateway's connection history for that day. In the first month the job found gaps on about two percent of shelves, almost all of them caused by a gateway bug that dropped the last partial frame before a modem reset. By the end of the parallel run the rate of unexplained differences had fallen below one shelf in fifty thousand, and the authority agreed to stop collecting cards.

The parallel run could only test what the trains happened to do, so we also built a replay rig. It consists of a rack of twelve small computers, each emulating five gateways, that can play back recorded traffic at up to eight times real speed and inject faults on request: dropped connections, duplicated frames, clocks that jump forwards or backwards, and coverage gaps of a chosen length. Every change to the ingest or storage code runs against the rig for six hours before it is deployed. The rig reproduced the reverse-order replay incident described in section 10 within minutes once we knew to look for it, which is more than can be said for the review that approved the firmware update in the first place.

Failure testing of the storage layer is done on the production cluster itself, during the scheduled maintenance window on the first Tuesday of each month. An engineer removes power from one storage node, waits until the placement service has finished re-replicating its shelves, and then restores it, recording how long each phase took and how much query latency rose in the meantime. Twice a year the whole western or eastern hall is isolated from the network for an hour. These exercises are unpopular with the control room, which is warned in advance, but they are the only evidence we have that the hall-failure behaviour described above works as written, and on two occasions they exposed problems that no test environment had shown: a firewall rule that blocked repair traffic between the halls, and a query node that kept sending sub-queries to the missing hall until its connection pool timed out.

Correctness of query results is checked differently. A small set of reference queries, chosen to cover sealed and unsealed shelves, summary columns and the batch queue, runs every hour against a frozen copy of one week of data whose answers were computed independently when the copy was made. A wrong answer pages the on-call engineer immediately, on the reasoning that a fast system returning wrong temperatures is worse than a slow one.

## 9. Operating the service

The service is run by a team of four engineers who share an on-call rotation with the authority's network group. Most alerts come from a small set of signals that we chose after the first incident described below: the age of the oldest unacknowledged frame on each ingest node, the size of each write-ahead log, the backlog of shelves waiting to be sealed, the fraction of each node's seek budget in use, and the number of digest mismatches found in the past week. Each signal has a threshold that pages the on-call engineer and a lower one that opens a ticket for the next working day. We deliberately avoided alerting on processor or memory use, which fluctuate widely without consequence and in the early months produced more noise than insight.

Upgrades are rolled out one hall at a time, and within a hall one node at a time, with a pause of at least an hour between nodes. Storage nodes are drained before an upgrade: the placement service stops directing queries to them, waits for in-flight reads to finish, and only then allows the process to restart. Ingest nodes are not drained, because their gateways reconnect on their own; instead, we upgrade them during the quietest hour of the night, between two and three in the morning, when fewer than a fifth of the trainsets are in service. A full upgrade of the cluster takes about two days, which is slow, but in two years no upgrade has caused an outage visible to users.

Capacity reviews happen every quarter. The review compares the seek model's projections with what the nodes actually did, checks the growth of sealed data against the purchase plan, and looks at the queries that spent the longest time in the batch queue. The last three reviews each found that the model overestimated seek demand by between six and nine percent, mostly because index caching works better in practice than the model assumes. We have left the overestimate in place as a safety margin rather than correct it.

## 10. Incidents

Two incidents in the first two years shaped the current design more than any requirement. The first happened eleven weeks after launch, when a firmware update on the trainsets changed the order in which gateways replayed buffered data after a tunnel. Instead of sending the oldest readings first, the new firmware sent them newest first. The in-memory buffers at the time were sorted structures, and each ingest node, receiving readings in reverse order for thousands of series at once, kept inserting at the front of them. The cost of insertion grew until frames took longer to process than they took to arrive. Acknowledgements slowed, gateways timed out and reconnected, and for about twenty minutes the control room saw no live data from the eastern half of the fleet. Nothing was lost, because every unacknowledged frame stayed in a gateway's buffer, but the control room had to fall back to radio reports. The fix was to replace the sorted buffer with an append-only buffer that is sorted when it is flushed, which made ingest cost independent of arrival order.

The second incident was slower and quieter. Over several months the weekly digest checker began reporting mismatches on a small number of unsealed shelves, about one in twenty thousand. Investigation showed that two storage nodes in the western hall were silently losing the contents of a drive's volatile cache during brief power dips, so a segment written just before such a dip could come back incomplete. The digests caught it, and the shelves were repaired from other replicas, but it took six weeks to identify the cause because the mismatches never repeated on the same shelf. We now disable volatile write caches on every drive, and each segment carries a checksum that a node verifies on every read, not only during the weekly check.

## 11. Access and security

Tessaline holds no personal data, but it holds data that matters for safety investigations, so access is controlled and logged. Engineers and planners authenticate through the authority's directory and receive read access to the subsystems their role covers. Research partners receive a separate account restricted to anonymised trainset identifiers and to the batch queue. Every query, including the series it touched and the number of bytes it read, is written to an audit log that is itself stored in Tessaline, in a dedicated set of shelves with a ten-year retention. Writes are only accepted from gateways that present a certificate issued during the refit, and certificates are rotated whenever a gateway is replaced.

## 12. Cost

The hardware budget was the requirement that worried the project most at the start, and it is the one that has needed the least attention since.
